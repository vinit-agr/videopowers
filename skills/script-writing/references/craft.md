# Craft: writing to be said

Every rule here holds for any speaker in any niche; one speaker's habits
(sentence length, signpost phrases, sign-offs, digits or words for numbers)
go in their speaker file.

A viewer hears each line once, at the speaker's pace, with no headings and
no rewind. Write for that listener. Sources: broadcast-news guides (Mervin
Block's tips, Jonathan Kern's NPR guide), speechwriting (the Congressional
Research Service guide, Peggy Noonan, Ted Sorensen), plain-language guides
(digital.gov, GOV.UK), YouTube practitioners (the MrBeast production memo;
Parker and Stone's "but / therefore"), and the general lessons from a
speaker's edits to generated drafts across five videos.

The examples below use made-up businesses. Each rule ends with the line used
in the self-check.

## Sentences

**1. One idea per sentence.** The start of a long sentence has faded by the
time a listener reaches its end.
- Before: "We cut onboarding from six weeks to two, which freed up the team, who then rebuilt the pricing page."
- After: "We cut onboarding from six weeks to two. That freed up the team. So they rebuilt the pricing page."
- Check: *Does any sentence carry two ideas joined by "which" or "who"?*

**2. Finish every phrase.** A sentence that ends on a bare noun or a
clipped verb sounds cut off. Add the object, the unit or who it's for.
- Before: "It took less than a minute. Cuts costs."
- After: "It took less than a minute to set up. It cuts your delivery costs."
- Check: *Does any sentence end on a bare noun, a dangling verb, or a fragment with no verb?*

**3. Put the word that matters at the end.** Spoken English stresses the
end of a sentence, so that's where the listener expects the news.
- Before: "Speed is the thing that matters most to buyers."
- After: "What buyers care about most is speed."
- Check: *Does each key sentence end on its strongest word, not on "it", "that" or a time phrase?*

**4. Active voice, a person who acts.** The listener hears who did what, in
order.
- Before: "The price increase was approved by the board."
- After: "The board approved the price increase."
- Check: *Any "was … by"?*

**5. Say who said it first.** The listener needs to know whose claim it is
before hearing it.
- Before: "Half of all leads go cold within an hour, according to one study."
- After: "One sales study found that half of all leads go cold within an hour."
- Check: *Does any source come after its claim?*

## Words

**6. Use the words people say, not the words they write.** "Buy", not
"purchase"; "but", not "however"; verbs, not "-tion" nouns.
- Before: "We made a decision to conduct an analysis of utilisation."
- After: "We decided to look at how much it's used."
- Check: *Any word you'd never say across a table? Any "make a decision", "conduct", "utilise"?*

**7. Name the thing in full the first time.** Say "website visitors", not
"visitors"; "independent bakery", not "shop". The full name tells the right
viewer the video is for them. After that, a shorter form is fine.
- Check: *Is each key noun named in full the first time it's said?*

**8. A technical term gets its plain meaning straight after it.**
- Example: "Our churn, the share of customers who leave each month, fell by a third."
- Check: *Is every term a newcomer might not know explained once?*

**9. Talk to the viewer as "you"; the people they serve are third person.**
Never make the viewer play their own customer.
- Before: "Imagine you're a patient searching at night…" (to an audience of clinic owners)
- After: "Imagine one of your patients searching at night. She finds your website…"
- Check: *Is "you" always the viewer, in their own role?*

## Numbers

**10. Round, give the unit, and give the direction.** Say what a number
measures before the number, round odd figures, and say which way it moved.
- Before: "Revenue: 23.7 percent, 4.12."
- After: "Revenue went up by almost a quarter, to just over four million dollars."
- Check: *Does every number have its unit, a direction word if it changed, and no needless decimals?*

**11. One number per sentence, and few per video.** A list of figures is a
table, not a sentence. Put dense numbers on screen instead.
- Check: *Any sentence with two or more numbers?*

**12. End a fact with what it means for the viewer.** The number alone
isn't the point; the cost or the gain to them is.
- Before: "Forty percent of trials never log in twice."
- After: "Forty percent of trials never log in twice. That's almost half the people you paid to bring in."
- Check: *Does each key fact end with what it costs or gives the viewer?*

## Flow

**13. Say the signposts out loud.** A listener can't see headings. Tell them
where they are: "That's the problem. Here's what fixed it."
- Check: *Does each chapter start with a spoken line that says where we are?*

**14. "But" and "so", not "and then".** Cause and turn make a story; a list
of events doesn't.
- Before: "We launched, and then we got users, and then we raised prices."
- After: "We launched. But nobody paid. So we raised prices, and that's when serious buyers showed up."
- Check: *Any run of "and then"?*

**15. Mix lengths; land the point short.** A short line after a longer one
gets the emphasis.
- Example: "We spent a year building features and pitching every investor who'd listen. None of it mattered."
- Check: *Is there a run of five sentences of the same length?*

**16. Repeat the key claim, not filler.** Bring the main point back in the
middle and at the end, in new words. Don't loop the same word ("so",
"today", "really") or echo a number as a lone word ("Zero.").
- Check: *Any word used three times in a paragraph without a reason? Any one-word echo?*

**17. Every open loop is closed.** If you promise "I'll show you at the
end", show it. A teaser that never pays off breaks trust.
- Check: *List every promise and open question; is each paid off?*

## What sounds written, not said

**18. Literal over clever.** Lines that work through rhythm, a neat
symmetric pair ("It's not about X. It's about Y."), a callback flourish or a
literary scene sound composed. Say the plain fact. One landing line per
video is plenty.
- Before: "The first three give a time. The last two give an answer."
- After: "Three of these tell the visitor when they'll hear back. The other two answer the question right away."
- Check: *Any line that sounds good but says less than a plain line would?*

**19. No warm-up, no asides, no self-doubt.** Cut "before we start", notes
on how the research was done, and lines like "I might be wrong about this".
One honest note per set of numbers is enough; the speaker's standing is
never hedged.
- Check: *Any sentence about the video, the method or the speaker's doubts that the viewer doesn't need?*

**20. No parentheses, abbreviations or symbols the speaker must translate.**
Brackets can't be said; "e.g." and "vs." get said wrong.
- Check: *Any brackets, abbreviations with full stops, or symbols other than % and $?*

## Respect

**21. Hedge claims about the viewer's business, not the main argument.**
"Your office might be closed at 2am" respects that you don't know their
business; "this is the problem" stays firm.
- Check: *Is any claim about the viewer's own business stated as certain?*

**22. Gentle words for sensitive topics.** Use the softer phrase when it's
just as clear ("hurting themselves", "a serious illness").
- Check: *Any harsh word where a gentler, equally clear one exists?*

**23. The speaker's own life is theirs to write.** Their self-introduction,
their stories, their opinions and their results come only from the speaker
file or the outline. If neither has them, leave a marker
(`[⚠ speaker: one line on what you've seen work here]`).
- Check: *Is any personal story, opinion or credential invented?*
