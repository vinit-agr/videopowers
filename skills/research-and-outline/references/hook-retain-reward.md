# Hook, retain, reward

From Alex Hormozi's *$100M Leads*: the **content unit** is hook → retain →
reward, and "to create a longer piece of content, we simply link content
units together." A 10-minute video is several units: the whole video is
one, and each chapter can be a small one.

- **Hook** earns attention: "They cannot be rewarded unless we first get
  their attention."
- **Retain** keeps it. "My favorite driver of retention is curiosity."
- **Reward** pays it back: "matching or exceeding their expectations when
  they decided to consume your content", with high value per second.

## The hook

A checklist, not a formula. Eight elements; each has a one-line test.

| Element | Required? | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Call-out** | yes | Would the right viewer think "this is for me" in the first sentence, in their own words? |
| **Surprise** | when there's a real one | Is there a fact that makes them stop: "wait, really?" Never invented. |
| **Proof** | yes | Is there a number, a credential or first-hand experience that earns trust? |
| **Stakes** | yes | Do they hear what it costs them (people, money, time) to get this wrong? |
| **Open loop** | yes | Is there one specific question left open that only the video answers? |
| **Promise** | yes | Do they know what they'll know or be able to do by the end? |
| **Plan** | when it helps | One line on how the video goes. Never a list of every section. |
| **Disqualifier** | when it helps | Who it's not for. Can go at the end of the hook, or be left out. |

**Order follows the awareness step.** A problem video (steps 1–2) may open
on a moment the viewer recognises and give the call-out second. Options and
buying videos usually open on the call-out.

**Length:** about 150–200 spoken words, about a minute. Over that, cut.

**Never a table of contents on its own.** "In this video I'll cover A, B
and C" is a plan, not a hook. A promise is about what the viewer gets; a
list of sections is about the video.

### Example (a fictional company that sells bookkeeping software to dental practices)

> **[Call-out]** If you run a dental practice with two or three dentists,
> and you still close the month in a spreadsheet, this is for you.
> **[Surprise]** Most practices don't lose money on the big bills. They
> lose it on the small refunds nobody posts back. **[Proof]** We looked at
> twelve months of books from practices that use our software, and the
> pattern was the same in almost all of them. **[Stakes]** Every refund
> that isn't posted makes your month look better than it is, until the
> tax bill arrives. **[Open loop]** There's one report that shows the
> problem in under a minute, and I'll show it to you at the end.
> **[Promise]** By the end of this video, you'll know where the missing
> money hides, and how to check your own books this week.

About 120 words: shorter than the 150–200 target, with room for a plan or
a disqualifier.

Named example of a call-out, from Tars (behavioral health): "Let's say you
run an addiction treatment center or an outpatient mental health clinic."

## Retain

- Curiosity drives it. Each chapter opens a question the viewer wants
  answered.
- Three devices:
  - **lists:** flexible, less explicit reward;
  - **steps:** "actions that must be done in a specific order to get a
    result"; less flexible, more explicit reward;
  - **stories:** a person, a moment, what happened.
- Use "but" and "so" between chapters, not "and then".
- Announce what's coming only when it helps the viewer follow, and never
  as the hook.

## Reward

- Match or beat the promise. If the hook promised three numbers, the video
  gives three numbers.
- Pay off every open loop, ideally the main one near the end.
- High value per second. Cut anything the story doesn't use.
- One thing the viewer can do **the same day**.

## Each chapter as a small unit

Where it comes naturally, a chapter is its own unit:
- it opens with its own question (its **mini hook**);
- holds attention through the chapter;
- pays off before the next chapter starts (its **reward**);
- then hands over with a small hook ("Okay, so it works. But what does it
  cost?").

This keeps people watching, and it makes each such chapter easy to cut
later into a stand-alone clip (30–90 seconds), a post or a carousel.

**Not forced.** Chapters like "who is speaking" have no mini unit. Mark
each chapter **stands alone: yes / no**. For each **yes**:
- write the clip's own first line, so it makes sense with no context;
- check the chapter never leans on what came before ("as I said", "remember
  the number from the start"). If it must, it doesn't stand alone.
